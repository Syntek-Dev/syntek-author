## Land and livelihood

The ford-keepers live where the river can be crossed on foot, and their living is the crossing.
They guide travellers, carts and cattle over the fords for a toll, fish the pools, and cut willow and alder on the banks.
Their materials are wood, cord and river stone; they have no clay worth firing and make no ink.
The tool every adult carries is a short knife, used for fish, rope, and the marks cut into wood.
Each ford is marked by an alder post on either bank, and the marks on those posts are the only writing they have: short, straight or gently curved cuts, because a knife in wet wood will not make a loop.

## Values and taboos

What they prize above everything is knowing the water: where it is safe, how it changes with the season, the rain and the hour.
Their speech is richest there, with many words for water by its state and speed, for the river bed by what lies on it, and for footing; that is where their language's vocabulary must run deepest.
The worst shame is to lose someone at a crossing, whether a traveller or kin.
To cross at night without calling across the water first is forbidden, because nobody would know to look for you.

## Beliefs and rites

They hold that the river remembers everyone who has crossed it, and that it forgets the careless first.
A child is carried across the home ford in its first spring, and the dead are set on the water below the last ford.
Prayers and the words of the rites keep older forms of words that nobody uses in daily speech, so the culture has a sacred register drawn from the older stage of its language.

## Power and kinship

Each ford belongs to a kin-group, reckoned through the mother, and its eldest member is its ford-keeper.
Ford-keepers settle disputes among the families who share their crossing; no one rules the whole river.
A ford-keeper is addressed by title and the name of the ford, never by given name, and a child speaks to any adult with a polite form; their speech marks rank in how people are addressed.

## Customs

Anyone about to cross calls across the water first, and waits for an answer.
A stranger who has crossed is fed before being asked anything, because the river has already tested them.
Two families in a quarrel walk the ford together, hand in hand, and talk on the far bank.

## Naming customs

A child's given name joins two short words, one of them about water, chosen by the mother's kin.
On taking charge of a crossing, a ford-keeper adds the ford's name after their own and keeps it for life.
Names are short, open, and easy to call across running water; a name that cannot be shouted over the river is not given.

## Variety and neighbours

The young of the busiest ford want a timber bridge, which would end the toll and the ford-keepers' authority, and the elders resist it.
Upstream families, nearer the hill-folk, borrow more of their words than the downstream families do.
The hill-folk and the marsh-dwellers are trading partners, not enemies.
