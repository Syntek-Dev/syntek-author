# FICTION.md — comprehension in a novel

A novel's reader knows only what the pages so far have told them, and nothing the story bible
knows. This pass reads as that reader: is it clear who, where and when; can they tell the
characters apart; can they follow an invented word from its context; is a mystery an intrigue or
a muddle?

## Paths and unit

- **The unit** is a chapter: `manuscript/src/NN-kebab-title/NN-kebab-title.md`.
- **The pass** is step 7 of `manuscript/workflows/05-review-a-chapter/`.
- **Extra reads:** `manuscript/docs/reference/scene-craft.md` (orientation); the chapter's brief,
  including its `## Continuity facts`; `planning/src/causality.md`, for what the story deliberately
  withholds; `world/src/names-register.md`.
- **The audience** in `.claude/CLAUDE.md` Section 1 is one of middle grade, young adult or adult.

## Additions to the steps

- **Step 1 — also:** what the reader knows is the promoted text of the earlier chapters in reading
  order, never the story bible, the character files or the plan. The bible always knows more than
  the reader.
- **Step 1 — also:** each audience's reading level: middle grade wants short sentences, clear cause
  and a named feeling; young adult and adult readers take more inference, but never inference about
  who is speaking or where they are.
- **Step 3 — also:** every invented word and name the reader meets for the first time: can its
  meaning, or at least its kind (a person, a place, a thing), be inferred from the sentence around
  it? Report the ones that cannot. Paragraphs crowded with invented words are `flow`'s density
  check; this pass reports the individual words the reader cannot place.
- **Step 4 — also:** information the story withholds on purpose (a planted mystery recorded in the
  brief or in `planning/src/causality.md`) is not a leap. The test is whether the reader knows what
  they do not know: intrigue is a question the reader can name; confusion is not knowing what
  happened.
- **Step 5 — also:** within the first lines of every scene the reader knows whose point of view this
  is, where and when; after a scene break, they know how much time has passed. Several characters
  introduced at once, or two names that look or sound alike, are reported (look-alike names are
  `create-name`'s to resolve).

## Domain rules

- **Never explain a deliberate mystery.** Report possible confusion, and let the author decide
  whether it is the confusion they intend.
- **Read the chapter as part of the book,** not as a standalone: a name introduced three chapters
  earlier and not seen since may need a reminder.
- **Dialogue that informs the reader of what both speakers already know** ('As you know, our
  father…') is a comprehension remedy gone wrong; never suggest it.

## Examples

> **Blocking.** `opening`, the first eight lines: two unnamed figures on a riverbank at night. The
> reader cannot tell whose point of view it is until line 9. Suggested remedy: name Maren within the
> first lines.

> **Friction.** `the-turn` line 6: 'the warden's mark' appears once, never explained, and the outcome
> of the scene depends on it. Suggested remedy: one clause of context where it first appears.

> **Not a finding.** Why Tam will not cross at night is withheld, and the brief records it as a
> planted question paid off later.
