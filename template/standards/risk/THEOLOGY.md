# THEOLOGY.md — honesty and contested ground

**Last Updated**: <%DATE%> **Version**: 0.1.0 **Maintained By**: <%AUTHOR_NAME%>
**Language**: British English (en_GB)

The risks particular to a theology project, read with `risk.md`. `structure-review` checks them
through its risk lens, `tradition-check` applies rule 2, and `steelman` applies rule 1 alongside
`standards/method/THEOLOGY.md`. The author's own stake in the argument is governed by that
method file (rule 8), not restated here.

Dates DD/MM/YYYY.

---

## 1. Examine the practice; never despise the practitioner

**Requirement.** Where the work examines something readers do, believe or lead, it is written so
that a reader who does it would recognise themselves and keep reading. Sneering, knowing asides
and pity are defects, not style. The test is not 'is this true?' but 'would someone who does this
recognise themselves in it, and still keep reading?'

**State permissive answers as plainly as restrictive ones.** A work that hedges when it approves
and speaks clearly when it disapproves has a thumb on the scale.

**Why this rule exists.** The readers most affected by the argument are the ones it most needs to
reach; a paragraph that makes them defensive has failed however well it is turned.

---

## 2. A tradition is described as its holders would recognise it

**Requirement.** A claim about what a church, denomination or tradition teaches or practises
cites a source its own members would accept (its confessions, its recognised teachers, its own
documents) and a date. A tradition is never characterised by its worst representative, and a
named congregation or leader is mentioned only on a public, checkable basis (`risk.md` rule 1).

**Why this rule exists.** A caricature of another tradition tells its members the argument was
not made in good faith, and they are right.

---

## 3. What is heard in pastoral confidence never enters the work

**Requirement.** Anything the author learned in pastoral care, in confession, in a safeguarding
disclosure or in a small group never appears in the work, even anonymised, unless the person
concerned has given explicit, recorded consent (`risk.md` rule 3). Where an illustration is
needed, it is invented and says so, or drawn from a published source.

**Why this rule exists.** Members of a congregation recognise each other in details no outsider
would notice; anonymity that holds for a stranger fails among the people who were there.

---

## 4. Children and the vulnerable are protected, never illustrations

**Requirement.** No identifying detail of a child or an adult at risk appears in the work, from
the author's ministry or anywhere else. Safeguarding failures are discussed from public sources
(inquiry reports, published reviews) with their dates, never from what the author heard.

**Why this rule exists.** Safeguarding is a legal duty as well as a pastoral one, and a detail
that identifies a child cannot be withdrawn once printed.

---

## 5. Hard counsel carries a route to help

**Requirement.** Where the work addresses a reader's sin, grief, doubt or harm, the passage
leaves them able to seek help: it names, in plain prose, the kind of person or service to turn
to. Any named organisation or contact is verified and dated (`standards/method/method.md`
rule 3), never recalled.

**Why this rule exists.** A passage that convicts without pointing anywhere leaves the reader
alone with it; guilt that has nowhere to go turns into hiding.
