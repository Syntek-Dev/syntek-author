# FICTION.md — depiction and real people

**Last Updated**: <%DATE%> **Version**: 0.1.0 **Maintained By**: <%AUTHOR_NAME%>
**Language**: British English (en_GB)

The risks particular to a work of fiction, read with `risk.md`. `structure-review` checks them
through its risk lens; `fact-check` applies rule 3's sourcing to any real-world detail;
`draft-section` writes to them. Invention does not exempt a work from harm: a story can defame,
endanger or exploit as surely as an essay.

Dates DD/MM/YYYY.

---

## 1. Depiction is a choice, and its handling is recorded

**Requirement.** Depicting harm is not endorsing it, but how it is depicted is the author's
decision, made knowingly. The subjects that need a content note are recorded once in
`.claude/MEMORY.md` `## Sensitivities`; whether the work carries content notes, and where, is an
`AUTHOR TO CONFIRM` decision recorded there too.

**Why this rule exists.** Readers who need a warning need it before the scene, not after, and a
decision taken scene by scene drifts.

---

## 2. Self-harm and suicide follow safe-messaging guidance

**Requirement.** A depiction of self-harm or suicide gives no method detail, does not present it
as a solution or as romantic, and is checked against the recognised safe-messaging guidance for
the jurisdiction the work is published in, cited and dated (`VERIFY` until checked).

**Why this rule exists.** Method detail and glamorised portrayals measurably raise risk for
vulnerable readers; the guidance exists because the harm has been studied.

---

## 3. Real people and real places are not defamed

**Requirement.** A real living person never appears doing or saying anything they did not
publicly do or say; where the story needs such a figure, it is invented, and checked not to
resemble a real one by name, office or circumstance. A real place may host invented events, but
a real, identifiable business, institution or official there is never shown in a damaging light.
Real-world detail (law, medicine, history, geography) is checked like any other claim.

**Why this rule exists.** A fictional frame does not protect against a defamation claim when a
reader can identify the person, and a wrong real-world detail breaks the reader's trust in the
invented ones.

---

## 4. Representation is researched, not assumed

**Requirement.** A character from a community, culture, faith or condition the author does not
share is researched from that community's own published voices, recorded in
`research/src/setting/`. Whether to engage a sensitivity reader is an `AUTHOR TO CONFIRM`
decision recorded in `.claude/MEMORY.md`. Authenticity markers (dialect, customs, slurs used by
characters) are never invented.

**Why this rule exists.** A character built from assumptions reads as a stereotype to exactly
the readers who would recognise the real thing.

---

## 5. Quoted material needs permission

**Requirement.** Every epigraph, song lyric, poem or extended quotation from a work still in
copyright is logged in `research/src/permissions.md` with its source and the permission status,
and stays flagged until permission is granted or the material is cut.

**Why this rule exists.** Even a few lines of a lyric can require permission and a fee; finding
out at the copy-edit stage can force a rewrite of the scene that depends on it.

---

## 6. Lived experience behind the story is shaped, never pasted

**Requirement.** Real events from the author's life or other people's that the story draws on
are transformed until the people involved could not be identified by those who know them, unless
they have given explicit, recorded consent (`risk.md` rule 3).

**Why this rule exists.** The people closest to an event recognise it in details a stranger would
never notice, and a novel can damage a relationship as surely as a memoir.

---

## 7. Real languages lend their sound, never their words

**Requirement.** An invented language, name or script may take the feel of a real one: its
sounds, syllable shapes, rhythm and structure. It never lifts that language's words, sacred
terms or holy names, and never caricatures it. Taking the feel of a minority, Indigenous or
sacred language is acceptable; lifting its vocabulary or sacred terms is flagged to the author
with an alternative. Every claim about a real language is researched and cited in
`research/src/setting/`, never asserted from memory, and a deliberate echo of a real word
records the word, its verified meaning and its source. A prominent invented word or name is
checked for unintended meanings in the languages it is modelled on and in English, slang
included. `create-name` and the language-building skills raise these points when a model or a
name is chosen; the sensitivity pass, where the project has one, checks the prose.

**Why this rule exists.** Speakers recognise their own language at once. A sacred term used as
decoration, or a language played for laughs, wounds them and reflects on the book; a 'fact'
about a real language remembered rather than checked is often wrong; and a false friend is found
by the first reader who knows the language.

---

## 8. A hostile people is never a real people in disguise

**Requirement.** Where the story casts a people as hostile or 'evil', nothing about them (looks,
customs, names or language) is modelled on a real ethnic group. Their language takes its models
from ancient or extinct languages, or from blends, never from one living group's language, and
harsh-consonant stereotypes are named and avoided. The case is flagged to the author every time
a people, a language model or a name style is proposed for them, with alternatives, and the
outcome is recorded in the people file's depiction note.

**Why this rule exists.** Readers carry the association back to real people: an enemy who looks
or sounds like one real group teaches readers to see and hear that group as the enemy, whatever
the author intended.
