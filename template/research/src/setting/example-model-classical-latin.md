## What is true

The standard spelling did not mark which vowels were long (Allen 1978, chapter 3, from the publisher's chapter summary).
The accent followed the 'Penultimate Law': in words of more than two syllables it fell on the second-to-last syllable when that syllable was heavy, and on the third-to-last when it was light (Allen 1978, chapter 5, from the publisher's chapter summary).
The points below are leads, not citations: each comes from Wikipedia alone, so its verdict is `thin` until a cited source confirms it.
Classical Latin had five vowel qualities, /i e a o u/, each of which could be short or long, so that length was a distinction of its own (lead: Wikipedia). <!-- VERIFY: five vowel qualities, each short or long; confirm in Allen (1978). -->
The letters ⟨qu⟩ wrote a single labialised velar consonant, /kʷ/ (lead: Wikipedia). <!-- VERIFY: ⟨qu⟩ as one sound /kʷ/; confirm in Allen (1978). -->
A stop followed by l or r between vowels usually went with the following syllable, so such clusters could open a syllable (lead: Wikipedia). <!-- VERIFY: stop-plus-liquid clusters open a syllable; confirm in Allen (1978). -->
At the end of a word, m was generally weakened from Old Latin onwards, leaving the vowel before it nasalised and lengthened (lead: Wikipedia). <!-- VERIFY: weakened final m; confirm in Allen (1978). -->

## Departures

Example Proto takes the five vowel qualities, /kʷ/ as one sound, and stop-plus-liquid clusters that open a syllable.
It deliberately leaves out vowel length and the weight-sensitive accent: its stress is simply on the second-to-last syllable, so that readers and narrators can find it without knowing which syllables are heavy.
It takes no Latin word, root or ending; every form is built from its own roots.
None of these departures touches the book's prose, so none is logged in `planning/src/continuity.md`.
