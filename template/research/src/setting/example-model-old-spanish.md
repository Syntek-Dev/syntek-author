## What is true

Between vowels, Latin's voiceless stops p, t and k were weakened in Spanish: they became voiced and then, as fricatives, [β ð ɣ], as in lupum > lobo 'wolf' and acutum > agudo 'sharp' (Mackenzie).
In the same development, the Latin ending of lupum gave the -o of lobo: the final m was lost and the vowel before it became o (Mackenzie's transcription of lupum as [ˈlopo]).
Latin f at the start of a word, followed by a simple vowel, mostly passed in Spanish through a stage as [h] before being lost, as in facere > hacer, filium > hijo and fabulari > hablar (Mackenzie).
Mackenzie notes that the initial f of words such as falso, figura and forma reflects learned influence.

The language is customarily called Old Spanish in its earliest documented form and until about the fifteenth century (lead: Wikipedia). <!-- VERIFY: the name and its end date; confirm in Penny (2002). -->
The first written evidence of the f to h change dates from 863 (lead: Wikipedia). <!-- VERIFY: the date 863; confirm in Penny (2002). -->
Learned words were borrowed later, straight from Latin, and did not go through the earlier changes, so a Latin word can survive twice: inherited Hernando beside learned Fernando, hondo beside fondo (lead: Wikipedia). <!-- VERIFY: the learned-borrowing pattern and both doublets; confirm in Penny (2002). -->

## Departures

Example Tongue takes the shape of four of these changes and their order is its own: final m lost, final u to o, voiceless stops voiced between vowels, word-initial f to h.
It takes the learned-borrowing pattern as hepom, a rite word borrowed back from the old language after the first three changes.
It takes the softening of b, d and g between vowels as allophones, written [β ð ɣ] in the surface form.
It keeps h as a sound, as Old Spanish did for a time, and does not take the later loss of h.
It takes no Spanish word, and none of the model's vowel changes.
None of these departures touches the book's prose, so none is logged in `planning/src/continuity.md`.
